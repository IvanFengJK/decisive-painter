What it is

Decisive Painter is a small painting toy. Decisions, not diffusion: a painter driven by a decision-maker that answers typed multiple-choice questions with a probability for every option. Code draws the answers. No image is generated by a model, and the decision-maker never sees or makes pixels. Everything runs in your browser.

The pictures are abstract, scene-like paintings: masses, bands, marks and colours arranged in a composition and a palette that suit your words. They are not pictures of the thing you typed.

Two ways to draw with a decision-maker

Gesture

The decision-maker picks one mark at a time from menus (which mark, where, how big, which medium, which palette colour, how to fill it, how heavy), in five phases from the background to the details, reading a short text that describes the painting so far. The odds panel shows the probabilities for every question of every mark. Styles:

  • Brush: each mark in its medium: pencil thin and grainy, pen solid thin ink, marker flat and translucent so it darkens where it overlaps, charcoal soft and smudgy, wash a soft-edged watercolour bloom.
  • Sketch: line only; shapes become outlines in ink.
  • Pixel: the marks snapped to a 64 by 64 grid in the painting's seven colours.

Probability map

The decision-maker gives odds over the six palette colours (dominant, secondary, accent, neutral, shadow, highlight) for every cell of a grid (16 to 48 cells across). The painting is those odds, drawn. Hover or tap a cell to see its odds; "Show certainty" makes sure cells bright and unsure cells dark. Styles:

  • Pixel: one block per cell, in its most likely colour or a seeded draw from its odds. The plainest view of the probabilities.
  • Impasto: thick oil paint. A smooth underpainting of each cell's average colour; a few thousand short strokes that follow its light and dark contours; every stroke's colour is drawn from the odds of the cell it starts in, with nearby strokes tending to draw alike so colour masses form; uncertain cells get thicker, more broken paint; the relief is lit from the upper left. It fades in when it is ready.

Who decides: Rules or Model

The badge above the painting always says exactly what decided the painting you are looking at.

Rules (the default): authored tables, no AI model and no download. About sixty scene types with their keywords and everyday words choose the recipe; per-mark preference tables (partly average choices measured from an open model's recorded paintings, see Licences) choose the marks; on the map, region tables over the composition's places, soft edges, a light-to-dark gradient, seeded smooth noise and a subject blob give every cell its odds.

Model (optional): press Download and enable model to download Qwen3-0.6B (a general small language model, int8, about 650 MB, Apache-2.0) and run it inside this tab, in a background worker: on WebGPU when your graphics card can run it (a short test question checks the answers first), otherwise on WebAssembly; the page says which it used, and why when it is not WebGPU. Nothing is downloaded before you press it, and your words are never sent to a server. It is not a trained decision model: it scores the lettered options of each question from its next-word probabilities, asks each question with the options in both orders and averages them, and no option may get more than 85%.

  • Gesture + Model: the model answers the six recipe questions once (mood, harmony, hue family, value key, saturation, composition; about 40 seconds on WebAssembly). The marks then follow the authored weights, and the badge says "recipe: model, marks: authored weights". Tick "Let the model choose the colour of a few marks too" (off by default) and, while the painting grows, it also chooses the colour role of one or two marks in each phase after the background (4 of 30 marks), each time reading a short summary of what is already on the canvas and the last two marks. That adds about 50 seconds on WebAssembly, and its colours usually look worse than the rules'. The odds cards of those marks are labelled "model", and the badge says "recipe and the colour of 4 of 30 marks: model; everything else: authored weights". If the model fails during a painting, the rules paint the rest of it and the badge says from which mark.
  • Probability map + Model: the model answers one colour question for each cell of a coarse grid (6x6 = 36 questions by default, about 6 minutes on a desktop CPU; or 4x4 or 8x8), with progress and Cancel. Its odds are interpolated smoothly to the full grid; "Blend with rules" mixes in the rules' map half and half. The badge says "Model on a 6x6 grid, interpolated" (or "... blended with rules"). The recipe on this tab comes from the rules' reading of your words.

Limits, honestly. In our earlier tests small models drifted towards austere, mid-value, near-monochrome palettes, and the rules usually looked better; a no-model control beat a 0.6B model. A small model deciding every cell is the hardest job here, so expect rough maps: it can read "upper part" and "lower part" a little, but it does not do per-cell colour well. 4-bit builds changed the model's top picks a lot, so only the 8-bit build is used. WebGPU and WebAssembly can answer close calls differently, so the same words and seed can give a different recipe on another device. If the model cannot load (no WebAssembly, not enough memory, offline), the page says so and the rules stay on. Shared links and saved paintings always open with the rules; the visitor can load the model again.

Decisions, not diffusion

A typed-question decision model reads a short text and answers questions of three kinds: choice (one option from a list), yes/no and score (a level on a scale), with a probability for every option. Code then picks from those odds and draws. A diffusion model generates the pixels of an image; here every decision is small, named and checkable, and any painting can be replayed exactly from its choices, which is what share links and Replay do. The Director interface is where any decision-maker plugs in: the rules, the model, or, under "Advanced" on the Gesture tab, an endpoint you run yourself (off by default; the page warns that your text would then be sent to it).

Same scene, seed, marks or grid, style and version give the same painting in the same browser engine. Impasto uses floating-point maths per pixel, so different browser engines may differ in tiny ways. Share links carry only the tab, style, recipe, seed and settings, never your words, an image or what a model answered.

What happens to what you type

  • It stays in this page. With the rules, the page makes no network requests while painting: no analytics, no cookies, no fonts or scripts from other sites. In Model mode the only requests are the model and its runtime (huggingface.co and its file CDN, and cdn.jsdelivr.net), and only after you press Download and enable model.
  • Every text first goes through input guardrails, before any decision-maker sees it:
    • clean-up: at most 80 characters, invisible and direction-changing characters removed, code and markup refused;
    • a hand-written filter for sexual content, any sexual content involving minors, graphic violence or gore, hate and slurs, self-harm, and targeting a named private person. It sees through letter swaps, spacing tricks, lookalike letters and repeated letters, and it lets ordinary scene words through (a blood orange sunset, a shooting star, a knife and fork on a table);
    • if a text is refused, the page shows one neutral sentence, paints nothing, logs nothing and sends nothing; a refused text never reaches the model;
    • a slot for an extra guard model, which may only add refusals and is switched off;
    • your words appear in one clearly marked, delimited field, and every answer must come from fixed lists, so instructions typed into the box are just words; the model's output is only ever a choice among the menu options, never run or shown as text.
  • "Save" keeps up to 20 paintings in this browser only (the recipe and a small thumbnail). Nothing is uploaded.
